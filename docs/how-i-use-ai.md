# My AI principles

*How I use AI to stay on top of work and life*

AI has become the team I always wanted: an executive assistant, a project manager, and a research collaborator. The job changes with the problem. It helps me keep a research team moving, investigate an idea, or decide what to do about a broken water heater. Family vacations count too.

I had plenty of work for this team. Thousands of unread emails were serving as my to-do list. So were items in my calendar. I was behind on referee reports and recommendation letters, and going into meetings trying to reconstruct where we had left off. I needed to be on top of my projects to keep everyone else on top of them.

AI even helped me write this essay. I've been trying to [teach it to write like me](build-your-own/voice.md). I dictated the ideas and examples, gave it my previous writing as a guide, and went back and forth over what it got wrong. AI wrote the first draft. It doesn't get my voice quite right, but iterating with it is much faster for me than writing from scratch. I get more done, and the basic idea gets out there.

This is one example of what the help looks like. At the very least, I get a first draft I can rewrite or a first answer I can work on.

There is a startup cost. I have to explain the job, provide the background, and correct what comes back. Getting some recurring tasks to work well took weeks. At first, it could take as long as doing the work myself. I made that investment because I knew I would need the help again.

The biggest gains have come from using independent agents and improving the way we work together. I ask several agents to develop approaches or challenge a draft, add my own judgment, and use what went wrong to make the next attempt better.

I discuss practical reservations about working this way in [AI Concerns](ai-concerns.md), including quality, confidentiality and whether the setup is worth the trouble.

## Build up incrementally, one task or project at a time

Try one or two useful tasks in the first couple of days. Get used to explaining what you want, reacting to what comes back, and trying again. Then [build from one task or project to the next](starter-kit/two-month-plan.md). Reproducing my entire setup is a terrible first assignment.

The jobs that have mattered to me suggest several places to begin:

- **Get obligations out of your inbox.** Working with AI, I shifted the commitments sitting in my unread email and calendar into Apple Reminders on my phone. This grew into [an executive assistant project](workflows/building-an-assistant.md) that helps me triage messages and reminders. A first attempt could be much smaller: take a few messages, identify what actually requires action, and put the confirmed tasks somewhere you will see them.

- **Get on top of one ongoing project.** My research projects have large teams, complicated histories, and results or decisions that need my attention every week. AI helps me prepare to give useful directions. You might begin with the notes from the last meeting and two recent updates, then ask what needs deciding at the next meeting and what information is missing.

- **Make the next recurring document easier.** A proposal or an application for ethics approval has to get done this week, but I know I will face a similar job again. I invest in the current document and in figuring out how to make the next one easier. Your starting point might be an old example, a new brief, and a draft you can edit.

- **Choose a problem from ordinary life.** Family vacations, household purchases, expenses, and tax-planning questions have all been useful applications for me. A replacement water heater or an electronic family calendar can be a worthwhile one-time investigation. You don't need to turn every purchase into a permanent system.

Choose something you need to do and can judge. Once you get comfortable, give AI more of the job or try a project with more moving pieces.

## Ask AI to help work out the approach

Whenever I catch myself thinking “I don’t know how to do this with AI,” that is usually the next thing I ask it. I don’t know how to teach it my voice. I don’t know how to organize this project. I don’t know what information it needs to help with my email. Those are things we can work out together.

I describe what I want and what matters to me. I don't need to arrive with a polished prompt or know the best method, because figuring out the method is part of the job. For questions about tools and setup, I ask it to look into current practices.

For an unfamiliar project, I sometimes ask several independent agents to propose an approach and tell me what they need to know first. I answer their questions, react to the proposal, and have a fresh set of agents review it critically and constructively. This was especially useful when I was getting started. I still do it when I have little idea how to begin.

When something breaks, I describe the problem and ask AI to investigate. I don't need to know which instruction is wrong or where the repair belongs. What I need is a diagnosis I can understand and react to.

## Make independent agents part of the way you work

For a difficult question, I want more than one agent’s first answer. I want different approaches, different arguments, and someone looking for what the others missed.

Sometimes I ask separate instances of the same AI to tackle a question. Sometimes I use different AIs. They work independently, then another agent brings the results together. [I call this a council](starter-kit/occasional.md), and I use councils for a great deal of my work. Asking one chat to play several characters doesn't give me the same independent attempts. I want to see useful disagreements, including the concern that only one reviewer raised.

I use these agents for different jobs. I can divide an investigation into separate questions, ask for competing ways to frame an argument, or have several reviewers examine the same plan. An alternative I reject can still help me figure out what I think.

Several agents can still repeat the same mistake. I ask them to examine disagreements and the reasons behind their answers, rather than treat a vote as the answer. I'm relying on them to do much of that investigation. I don't independently check everything they produce.

## Have a fresh council challenge the plan, then revise it

Once we have what looks like a good plan, I often ask [a fresh council to find what's wrong with it](starter-kit/occasional.md#review). I give feedback too, and that cycle of planning, criticism, and revision accounts for a large part of the improvement I get from these tools.

The sequence looks something like this:

1. Explain the job and have agents investigate or propose approaches independently.

2. Bring their findings together, retaining the disagreements that matter.

3. Add my judgment: what I actually want, which constraints they missed, and where the plan makes no sense to me.

4. Have fresh reviewers examine the revised plan, with the relevant evidence and constraints.

5. Revise the plan and use it for the job. Give feedback on what comes back, and return to the review when there is a problem worth another pass.

The fresh reviewers need the job, the evidence, and the constraints. They don't need the entire conversation that persuaded the first group. I want something I can act on: a bad assumption, a missing dependency, an alternative worth trying. They should also be free to say the plan looks sound.

I can often tell when a proposal misses the argument I want to make, a recommendation misunderstands my priorities, or a draft sounds nothing like me. I edit it or explain the problem. Work I can't judge is harder. Several reviewers help, but they don't make that difficulty disappear.

In my experience, this cycle makes an enormous difference to whether I can actually use the result (though I haven't measured the benefit of each additional review).

Another pass needs a job. Sometimes it's worth checking an important point again. Sometimes the agents are just rearranging the adjectives and it is time to get on with the work. Agreement doesn't make an answer true, and not every objection deserves a repair.

## Teach it with examples and corrections

A review improves the task in front of me. Examples and corrections can make the next one easier. A recommendation letter is a perfect snowflake, but it still has a structure and a voice. My old letters give AI something concrete to learn from. They can't decide where I place a new student relative to others I have recommended, or what I think is strongest about the student’s work. I have to supply those judgments.

Referee reports show how the process develops. I read the paper and dictate my comments. AI can help me examine a claim I am uncertain about, then turn my comments into a draft using my previous reports as examples. I edit that draft myself and say how I used AI. The confidentiality rules that apply to a report still govern what I can give a tool.

Finishing the report is also a chance to improve the next one. Correcting the current conversation doesn't necessarily change the next one. So I ask AI to compare my edited version with its draft and work out what it should do differently. Then I ask it to [save useful lessons](how-i-improve-the-work.md) in the instructions or examples it will use again.

I also ask where the lesson belongs. A correction to a project’s budget should stay with that project. A recurring problem with my voice might belong in the writing instructions. AI can help decide what to save and where. Otherwise, I'll be explaining the same mistake next time.

## Keep improving the system, including removing what no longer helps

Over weeks and months, I also use corrections to change how the whole project works. Call it recursive improvement: using AI to help improve the way I use AI.

My corrections change the instructions, examples, or organization it will use next time. Editing a paragraph doesn't retrain the underlying model.

Every so often, AI gets fixated on something I told it three months ago. Or it starts doing badly at a job it used to handle well. I ask it to diagnose the problem, sometimes with several independent opinions. We might clarify an instruction, move some information, or delete a rule. For a large setup, I take a broader look every few months.

Even the organization can become unnecessary. I used to put more effort into rolling summaries of research projects. As the tools improved, some of those summaries seemed less essential. More files and more instructions aren't automatically an improvement. When an old instruction or process is getting in the way, I ask AI to help me work out what has become unnecessary.

## Organization pays off, especially when you work in projects

[Each research project has a home](workflows/organize-projects.md), with its papers, correspondence and decisions. So do the committees I sit on, home repairs and family vacations. When I open a project, I want to pick up where we left off.

My projects in Codex largely reflect how I've organized my hard drive. I already had a reasonably organized collection of folders in Dropbox. One of the jobs I gave AI in the first months was helping me reorganize that Dropbox. It wasn't my first task.

I ask AI to help organize what it needs to know: what we are trying to do, who is involved, what we have decided, and what still needs attention. It needs to distinguish an old proposal from a current decision. Saving those decisions and the next actions makes it easier to return.

Being organized doesn't mean putting every old document into every conversation. Keep the important current context easy to find, with a way to reach the background when needed. A long collection of unrelated instructions can make the job harder.

A lot of my work now happens in desktop tools with access to those folders, though keeping related work together in a chatbot project is useful too. You can begin by supplying the notes and documents for one meeting, then ask what else is missing. You don't need to reorganize your whole drive first.

For a team meeting, I want to know what we were supposed to accomplish, what the new results mean, and what needs my attention. Preparation can turn into an investigation of a research question. Before exploring a new subject or deciding whether to write about it, I might want [a researched briefing](workflows/deep-research.md) of two pages, five, or twenty. Having the project’s history available helps me ask better questions without reconstructing everything each time.

## Choose how much access to give it

I'm relatively risk-accepting about this. I have connected more of my email, calendar, WhatsApp, and meeting transcripts than many people would be comfortable with. There is a large benefit to an assistant that can gather the relevant material itself. I spend less time finding, copying, and explaining it.

For me, the benefits outweigh the costs and risks. Part of my trust comes from the companies’ financial stake in protecting users. A serious security failure would be costly for them. That gives them a reason to invest in protection, without telling me whether a particular connection or permission is safe.

I try to make deliberate choices about what the tools can reach. Much of my private information has lived in Dropbox for years. I use Selective Sync to keep especially sensitive Dropbox folders off my computer. Research data stays in university Box, with selective access rather than general access. I also ask AI to help with periodic security audits: what can it reach, what is unnecessarily exposed, and what should change? An AI-assisted audit can help identify problems, but it can't certify that nothing has been missed.

I also need to be clear about what those precautions accomplish. Dropbox’s online-only setting can download a file when it's opened. Selective Sync removes selected folders from the local drive, but a connected cloud account can provide another access route. [Dropbox explains the difference](https://help.dropbox.com/sync/selective-sync-overview) and the [separate access granted to connected apps](https://help.dropbox.com/integrations/third-party-apps). Storage location, permissions, and connected services are separate questions. Working from files on my hard drive doesn't mean the model processes them only on my computer. For example, [Claude Code sends data to its model service](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/data-usage). Limits on model training, data retention, and access are different protections.

Permission to read information is also different from permission to send, change, or delete it. For ordinary email, I want to approve the actual message before it's sent. A rule in a project file is an instruction, not a technical limit that prevents an action. The risk is also broader than a company getting hacked. An instruction hidden in a web page or email can try to redirect an agent that reads it. The possibility matters more when the same assistant can read private material and communicate outside the project. [Anthropic describes this problem and the limits of its defenses](https://www.anthropic.com/research/prompt-injection-defenses).

There are also people whose risk I don't get to decide: research participants, colleagues, students, and anyone whose private information appears in a message or transcript. My willingness to take a risk doesn't settle their rights or the rules governing their data.

You will have to make your own choices about access. Begin with a few documents if that's what you're comfortable with. Expand when the benefit warrants it. My willingness to connect more of my life is a personal judgment.

## Decide which parts of the work to delegate

I also delegate different amounts of writing, depending on the job. AI can help draft routine emails and responses through several iterations, and I generally edit them myself. I spend less time polishing a casual reply. Proposals and applications for ethics approval can also start with a substantial AI draft, using my examples and refined by me.

For academic papers and most articles for a public audience, I generally want to do the writing myself. My professional reputation rests on this work, and I care about the argument and the wording. I also think a high AI share can undermine its credibility. This essay is an exception: I've asked AI to do more of the writing and explained that at the start.

There is still a great deal of help to ask for while I write. AI can locate a source, interrogate an idea, identify an objection, or help me think through a theoretical problem. It can research an unfamiliar subject so I can decide what I think about it. Delegating the investigation and criticism can leave more of my attention for the writing I want to do myself.

Those boundaries depend on the piece and what I want from it. The tool’s ability to write a draft doesn't settle how much I want to delegate.

## Use the help to get moving—and to put things down

One benefit is how much easier it is to begin. A coauthor sends back a paper, and revising it feels enormous. I can open the project, ask AI to read what has come back, and help me find a few concrete things to do first. As with this essay, even a bad first pass gives me something to react to. Arguing about what is wrong gets me moving.

The help is also available at odd moments. With my computer on and my project material accessible, I can be at the gym at eight, remember a meeting at nine-thirty, and dictate what I need prepared from my phone. It works on the briefing while I finish my workout. I can scan it before the meeting, or during it to check what we have missed. You do not have to work from the gym. For me, being able to hand over the preparation helps me put it down for a while.

The overwhelming personal achievement has been becoming less of a hot mess. I feel more on top of things. That has helped the people I work with too. If anything, I have more staff than before, and they use these tools as well.

There is a catch. Getting five projects moving is a very effective way to avoid spending two uninterrupted hours writing. AI can keep me with an argument by finding the information I need, or tempt me away to something easier.

Getting help with five projects still leaves me to decide which one deserves my afternoon.
