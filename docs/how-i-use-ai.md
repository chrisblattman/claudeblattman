# How I put AI to work

*How I use AI to stay on top of work and life*

I use AI for much of the help I always wanted from an executive assistant, a project manager, and a research collaborator. The role changes with the job. It can help me keep a research team on track, investigate an idea I am thinking of writing about, or work through a decision at home. Family vacations and broken water heaters count too.

I had plenty of work for this team. Thousands of unread emails were serving as my to-do list. So were items in my calendar. I was behind on referee reports and recommendation letters, and going into meetings trying to reconstruct where we had left off. I needed to be on top of my projects to keep everyone else on top of them.

The help goes well beyond administration. I can ask for an investigation of a research question, work through an argument, or prepare for a conversation in an unfamiliar area. I wanted people I could turn to for all these jobs long before I could organize or afford that much help. AI has made it possible to develop more of that help.

It takes work. I have to explain the job, provide the background, and correct the results. For some recurring tasks, getting the process right took weeks. At first, it took at least as long as doing the work myself. The benefit came from using that investment again.

Two practices have mattered especially: having independent agents challenge the work, and using what goes wrong to improve the way we work next time. I return to both throughout a project.

## Build up incrementally, one task or project at a time

Aim to try one or two useful tasks in the first couple of days. Get familiar with the tools and with the process of explaining a job, inspecting what comes back, and improving it. Then build from one task or project to the next. Getting accustomed to the approach is a more useful first ambition than reproducing someone else’s entire setup.

The jobs that have mattered to me suggest several places to begin:

- **[Get obligations out of your inbox.](workflows/building-an-assistant.md#2-inbox-triage-triage-inbox)** I was using unread email and calendar entries to remember things I needed to do. Working with AI, I shifted those commitments into Apple Reminders on my phone. This grew into an [executive assistant project](workflows/building-an-assistant.md) that helps me triage messages and reminders. A first attempt could be much smaller: take a few messages, identify what actually requires action, and put the confirmed tasks somewhere you will see them.
- **[Get on top of one ongoing project.](workflows/project-management.md)** My research projects have large teams, complicated histories, and results or decisions that need my attention every week. AI helps me prepare to give useful directions. You might begin with the notes from the last meeting and two recent updates, then ask what needs deciding at the next meeting and what information is missing.
- **[Make the next recurring document easier.](workflows/project-management.md#layer-4-proposals-and-reports-on-demand)** A proposal or an application for ethics approval has to get done this week, but I know I will face a similar job again. I invest in the current document and in figuring out how to make the next one easier. Your starting point might be an old example, a new brief, and a draft you can edit.
- **[Choose a problem from ordinary life.](workflows/executive-assistant.md)** [Family vacations](workflows/life/vacation.md), [household purchases](workflows/life/shopping.md), expenses, and [tax-planning questions](tax-workflow/index.md) have all been useful applications for me. A replacement water heater or an electronic family calendar can be a worthwhile one-time investigation. You do not need to turn every purchase into a permanent system.

Those are possibilities, not a required sequence. Choose something you need to do and can judge. As you get more comfortable, give the AI a larger part of the job or try a responsibility with more moving pieces.

## Ask AI to help work out the approach

Whenever I catch myself thinking “I don’t know how to do this with AI,” that is usually the next thing I ask it. I don’t know how to teach it my voice. I don’t know how to organize this project. I don’t know what information it needs to help with my email. Those are things we can work out together.

I describe what I want to accomplish and what matters to me. I do not need to arrive with a polished prompt or already know the best method. Working out the method can be part of the job I give it. When the question concerns tools or setup, I ask it to research current practices.

For a new project, I sometimes ask a council to suggest an approach and come back with questions for me. What does it need to know before it can make a useful recommendation? I answer, react to the proposal, and have a second council review it critically and constructively. This was especially helpful when I was first getting started with AI. I still use it when the task is unfamiliar and I have little idea how to begin.

This also applies when a workflow fails. I don’t need to know which instruction to rewrite or which file should hold it. I can describe what went wrong and ask the AI to investigate. I still need to judge the proposed diagnosis. That is one reason the next two habits matter so much.

## Make independent agents part of the way you work

[Multi-agent work](starter-kit/occasional.md#why-three-agents) is central to how I use AI. For difficult work, I would be much less comfortable relying on a single agent’s first answer. I want several attempts, different lines of investigation, and people—or their imperfect AI equivalents—looking for what the others missed.

Sometimes I ask several separate instances of the same AI to tackle a question. Sometimes I use [different AIs](build-your-own/ai-integration.md#why-mix-vendors). I call this a [council](starter-kit/occasional.md). Each can work independently before another pass compares the results. Useful disagreement gets preserved: one reviewer may spot a bad assumption that every other reviewer accepted.

There are two different jobs here. I might divide a large investigation into distinct questions, so agents can research different parts. Or I might give several agents the same plan to assess independently. Dividing the work broadens what gets examined. Independent reviews give me several chances to discover a problem. Asking one chat to play several characters does not give me the same separation.

Several agents can still repeat the same mistake. I ask them to examine disagreements and the reasons behind their answers, rather than treat a vote as the answer. I am relying on them to do much of that investigation. I do not independently check everything they produce.

## Have a fresh council challenge the plan, then revise it

The criticism comes after the apparent success. Once a council has helped me arrive at a plan, I often ask another fresh council to find what is wrong with it. I give feedback too. That cycle of planning, critical review, and revision is one of the most important parts of my approach.

The sequence looks something like this:

1. Explain the job and have agents investigate or propose approaches independently.
2. Bring their findings together, retaining the disagreements that matter.
3. Add my judgment: what I actually want, which constraints they missed, and where the plan makes no sense to me.
4. Have fresh reviewers examine the revised plan, with the relevant evidence and constraints.
5. Revise the plan and use it for the job. Give feedback on what comes back, and return to the review when there is a problem worth another pass.

The next reviewers need enough context to understand the job. They do not need to inherit the entire conversation that persuaded the first group. When reviewers find a problem, I want something I can act on: an unsupported claim, a missing dependency, an alternative worth trying, or a test that would tell us whether the plan works. They should also be able to tell me that the plan is sound.

My feedback is most useful when I can recognize what is wrong. A proposal can miss the argument I want to make. A recommendation can misunderstand my priorities. A draft can sound nothing like me. I can edit it or explain the problem. That is harder with work I cannot judge as readily. The review process helps, but it does not remove that difficulty.

This has been essential to getting difficult work to a standard I can use. I think it accounts for a large part of the difference between an impressive first answer and useful assistance. That is my experience, not a measured estimate of how much each additional review helps.

There is also a point where another review produces more wording and little new information. The useful question is what the next pass could discover or check. Reaching agreement is not, by itself, a reason to trust the answer. Nor does disagreement mean that every objection deserves a repair.

## Teach it with examples and corrections

Past work gives AI something concrete to learn from. A recommendation letter is a perfect snowflake, but it still has a structure and a voice. My old letters provide examples. They cannot decide where I place a new student relative to others I have recommended, or what I think is strongest about the student’s work. I have to supply those judgments.

Referee reports show how the process develops. I read the paper and dictate my comments. AI can help me examine a claim I am uncertain about, then turn my comments into a draft using my previous reports as examples. I edit that draft myself and say how I used AI. The confidentiality rules that apply to a report still govern what can be supplied to a tool.

Finishing the report is also a chance to improve the next one. I ask AI to compare my edited version with its draft and work out what it should do differently. Then I ask it to save useful lessons in the instructions or examples it will use again. Correcting the current conversation does not necessarily change the next one.

The lesson needs somewhere to live. A correction to this project’s budget belongs with its facts. A recurring problem with my voice might belong in the [writing instructions](how-i-improve-the-work.md#make-a-correction-useful-next-time). I ask AI to help work out [what to save and where](build-your-own/project-folders.md#build-your-first-file), so I do not have to keep explaining the same mistake. This is part of improving the way we work together.

## Keep improving the system, including removing what no longer helps

The immediate review cycle improves the task in front of me. A second kind of improvement happens over weeks and months, as corrections change how the project works next time.

Every so often, AI gets fixated on something I told it three months ago, or starts doing badly at a task it handled well before. I describe the failure and ask it to help diagnose the problem, sometimes with several independent opinions. The repair may be to clarify an instruction, change where information is stored, or delete a rule. For a large setup, I take a broader look every few months.

This is what I mean by recursive improvement: I use AI to help [examine and improve the way I use AI](how-i-improve-the-work.md#repair-the-way-you-work-together). My edits and complaints give it something to work with. We use them to change the instructions, examples, or organization of the project. The underlying model is not retraining itself every time I edit a paragraph.

Even the organization can become unnecessary. I used to put more effort into rolling summaries of research projects. As the tools improved, some of those summaries seemed less essential. More files and more instructions are not automatically an improvement. When an old instruction or process is getting in the way, I ask AI to help me work out what has become unnecessary.

## Organization pays off, especially when you work in projects

My projects in Codex largely reflect how I have organized my hard drive. I already had a reasonably organized collection of folders in Dropbox. One of the jobs I gave AI in the first months was helping me [reorganize that Dropbox](workflows/cloud-storage-guide.md). It was not my first task, and it is not a prerequisite for someone else’s first task.

Related work has a home. Each research project has its material together. So do committees, home repairs, and family vacations. The value comes from being able to find the relevant work, understand its current state, and return to it. A folder name alone does not accomplish that.

I ask AI to help organize the material for its use as well as mine: what we are trying to do, who is involved, where the work lives, what we have decided, and what still needs attention. I want it to distinguish an old proposal from a current decision. When a session ends, [preserving the important decisions and next actions](workflows/session-management.md) makes the next session easier to begin.

Being organized does not mean putting every old document into every conversation. Keep the important current context easy to find, with a way to reach the background when needed. A long collection of unrelated instructions can make the job harder.

A lot of my work now happens in desktop tools with access to those folders. Keeping related work together in a chatbot project is useful too. You can begin by supplying the notes and documents for one meeting, then ask what else is missing. You do not need to reorganize your whole drive first.

For a team meeting, I want to know what we were supposed to accomplish, what the new results mean, and what needs my attention. Preparation can turn into an investigation of a research question. Before exploring a new subject or deciding whether to write about it, I might want a researched briefing of two pages, five, or twenty. Having the project’s history available helps me ask better questions without reconstructing everything each time.

## Choose how much access to give it

I am relatively risk-accepting about this. I have connected more of my email, calendar, WhatsApp, and meeting transcripts than many people would be comfortable with. There is a large benefit to an assistant that can gather the relevant material itself. I spend less time finding, copying, and explaining it.

From my perspective, those benefits outweigh the costs and risks. Part of my trust comes from the companies’ financial stake in protecting their users. A serious security failure would be costly for them. That gives them a reason to invest in protection. It does not tell me that a particular connection, permission, or action is safe.

I try to make deliberate choices about what the tools can reach. Much of my private information has lived in Dropbox for years. I use Selective Sync to keep especially sensitive Dropbox folders off my computer. Research data stays in university Box, with selective access rather than general access. I also ask AI to help with periodic security audits: what can it reach, what is unnecessarily exposed, and what should change?

I also need to be clear about what those precautions accomplish. Dropbox’s online-only setting can download a file when it is opened. Selective Sync removes selected folders from the local drive, but a connected cloud account can provide another access route. [Dropbox explains the difference](https://help.dropbox.com/sync/selective-sync-overview) and the [separate access granted to connected apps](https://help.dropbox.com/integrations/third-party-apps). Storage location, permissions, and connected services are separate questions.

The same is true of permission to read information and permission to send, change, or delete it. For ordinary email, I want to approve the actual message before it is sent. [A rule in a project file is an instruction.](build-your-own/rules.md#rules-are-not-a-safety-system) It is not the same as a technical limit that prevents an action.

The risk is also broader than a company getting hacked. An instruction hidden in a web page or email can try to redirect an agent that reads it. The possibility matters more when the same assistant can read private material and communicate outside the project. [Anthropic describes this problem and the limits of its defenses](https://www.anthropic.com/research/prompt-injection-defenses).

Working from files on my hard drive does not mean the model processes them only on my computer. For example, [Claude Code sends data to its model service](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/data-usage). Limits on model training, data retention, and access are different protections.

There are also people whose risk I cannot decide for them: research participants, colleagues, students, and anyone whose private information appears in a message or transcript. My willingness to take a risk does not settle their rights or the rules governing their data. An AI-assisted audit can help identify problems. It cannot certify that nothing has been missed.

Readers will make different choices. You can try a task with a small set of supplied documents and expand access when the benefit warrants it. The point is to understand what you are granting and what you are gaining, rather than assume that my level of access should be yours.

## Decide which parts of the work to delegate

I draw different boundaries for different writing. For routine emails and responses, AI can help develop a draft through several iterations, and I generally edit it myself. I am less fastidious about a casual reply than about prose I expect people to read closely. Proposals and applications for ethics approval can also begin with a substantial AI draft, informed by my examples and refined by me.

I give AI very little of the writing itself in an academic paper or an article for a public audience. These are things my professional reputation rests on. I have the lowest tolerance for errors, and I care about the argument, the wording, and whether the piece feels like mine. A high AI share can also undermine the credibility of that work in a way I find less troubling for a routine administrative document.

There is still a great deal of help to ask for while I write. AI can locate a source, interrogate an idea, identify an objection, or help me think through a theoretical problem. It can research an unfamiliar subject so I can decide what I think about it. Delegating the investigation and criticism can leave more of my attention for the writing I want to do myself.

These are judgments about what I value and what the work requires. Having a tool that can produce a draft does not decide how much of that draft I want to use.

## Use the help to get moving—and to put things down

One benefit is how much easier it is to begin. A coauthor sends back a paper, and revising it feels enormous. I can open the project, ask AI to read what has come back, and help me find a few concrete things to do first. Even a bad first pass gives me something to react to. Arguing about what is wrong gets me moving.

The help is also available at odd moments. With my computer on and my project material accessible, I can be at the gym at eight, remember a meeting at nine-thirty, and dictate what I need prepared from my phone. It works on the briefing while I finish my workout. I can scan it before the meeting, or during it to check what we have missed. You do not have to work from the gym. For me, being able to hand over the preparation helps me put it down for a while.

The overwhelming personal achievement has been becoming less of a hot mess. I feel more on top of things. That has helped the people I work with too. If anything, I have more staff than before, and they use these tools as well.

There is a catch. AI makes it easy to get five projects moving, which can be a wonderful way to avoid spending two uninterrupted hours writing. It can help me stay with an argument by finding what I need. It can also tempt me away to something easier. I have been quite capable of maintaining my level of anxiety by taking on more things.

Getting help with five projects still leaves me to decide which one deserves my afternoon.
