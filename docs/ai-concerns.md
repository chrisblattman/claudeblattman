# AI Concerns

There are legitimate reservations about using AI at work. I won't try to answer all of them here, or settle the larger debates about jobs and society. [A few balanced places to read about those debates](#bigger-questions) are at the end. These are practical questions about the workflows on this site: getting useful help, deciding what to delegate, and making the investment worthwhile. My answers come mostly from experience, including some bets that may turn out to be wrong.

## “I'm getting slop.”

A polished draft that misses the point is still a bad draft, whether it comes straight from AI or a colleague passes it along.

On structured, recurring work, I think very high quality is possible. I've had good results with emails and project reports, and in my work, proposals and applications for ethics approval. These documents still require original ideas and difficult decisions. Their familiar structure gives AI a useful starting point. For a distinctive piece of writing, such as an op-ed or a book chapter, I'm much less satisfied with it producing the final prose. It helps me investigate ideas and work out the argument, then I do much more of the writing myself.

Getting good work takes an investment. I use agents that can read project files and carry a job through several steps. I give them earlier examples, explain what they got wrong, and work with them to improve the result.

Take a proposal. An earlier one shows AI the kind of document we need. It cannot tell the agent what we want to do this time, why we think it will work, or which difficult choices we have made. I set it up to ask me or a colleague for that missing information before drafting. Answering those questions is part of developing the proposal. It gets the thinking into the conversation before smooth prose can cover up what nobody has decided.

For a substantial task, I ask agents to develop approaches independently. I react to what comes back, explain what they misunderstood, and have fresh reviewers challenge the revised plan or draft. I want them to question my reactions too. [Independent attempts and fresh criticism](how-i-use-ai.md#make-independent-agents-part-of-the-way-you-work), alongside my own judgment, have made a large difference to the work I get. The result is a first draft I can refine instead of starting over.

My edits can also help the next task. I ask AI to compare my version with its draft and save useful lessons in the instructions or examples it will consult again. Correcting one conversation doesn't automatically improve the next one. Saving the lesson changes what it has to work with. It doesn't retrain the underlying model.

You can try a small version in a chatbot: give it a good earlier example and your rough points, then ask what it needs to know before drafting. Expect a conversation about what comes back.

??? question "What about invented facts, citations and confident mistakes?"

    I hear this concern less than I did a year or two ago. My impression is that the systems have improved, and people are learning to use them better. On the kinds of tasks my agents do well, I also feel I see fewer factual and citation errors than in first drafts I used to delegate to people. That's an impression, not a measured comparison.

    Mistakes still happen. I don't personally fact-check everything AI produces. For important work, I keep people involved. For less consequential work, I'm happy to accept some errors in return for getting much more done. I don't need every task to be error-free for the help to be valuable.

    Several agents can repeat the same mistake. Their agreement doesn't make a claim true. I can often tell when a proposal misses my point or a draft sounds nothing like me. Work I can't judge is harder, and adding reviewers doesn't make that difficulty disappear.

??? question "Will polished work make quality harder to distinguish?"

    This hasn't been a major problem for me so far. I also see room for AI to level the playing field by helping people consider things they would otherwise miss and learn from the process. Used poorly, it can encourage sloppy thinking.

    My guess is that polished applications and pitches will tell us less about who can do the job. We'll turn to other forms of assessment and put more weight on reputation and demonstrated ability to carry something through. That could make it harder for newcomers without a track record to distinguish themselves through writing alone, even when AI has helped them produce better work.

## “Will I lose my voice, originality or credibility?”

For routine documents, I don't think good AI-assisted work has to cost me any of those things. An email that expresses my thoughts in language I'm happy with can be good enough for its audience. I don't need to write every first draft myself.

I dictate my points and give AI examples of my writing. I ask it to use my ideas without adding its own. When I want new ideas, I ask for them separately and decide what to use. [Teaching it my voice](build-your-own/voice.md) produces a first draft that sounds enough like me to save a great deal of time. It still needs my judgment and edits.

For academic papers and most public articles, I want to do much more of the writing myself. My professional reputation rests on the argument and the wording. AI can help with research and criticism, but a draft I'd accept for an internal memo can take a lot of work before I want it to represent me in public.

The purpose and the rules matter. A school assignment meant to assess your own writing has a different standard from a routine report. This page and my principles essay are examples where I've chosen more AI assistance and disclosed it. The tool's ability to write a draft doesn't settle how much I want to delegate.

## “Can I give it confidential work?”

I'm not a security expert, and advice in this area is changing quickly. For proprietary business material, client information or colleagues' correspondence, I'd start with your organization's approved AI service. If your company doesn't have one, or you run a small business, look for a suitable business or enterprise arrangement and exercise caution.

Check which account you're using, how it's configured, and what information you're allowed to give it. [Approval comes with conditions](https://genai.uchicago.edu/generative-ai-tools/approved-and-restricted-ai-tools). A business label alone doesn't settle those questions. A promise not to train on your information also doesn't mean the provider [won't store it](https://privacy.claude.com/en/articles/7996866-how-long-do-you-store-my-organization-s-data).

If you bring another AI into the work, its account needs approval for the same material. A personal subscription doesn't override your organization's rules or requirements from a client, funder or journal. If work restricts agents, there are still ways to [learn with approved tools and personal projects](starter-kit/agents-at-work.md).

If you make these decisions for an organization, I think there are big advantages to giving people capable approved tools and as much practical freedom within them as possible. My worry is that restrictions which make the tools unhelpful encourage people to use unmanaged personal accounts. I'd much rather let them do ambitious work inside an arrangement the organization can manage.

Personally, I'm relatively risk-accepting. Connecting my email and project material lets the assistant find what it needs. I spend less time gathering and explaining it. For me, the benefits outweigh the disadvantages and risks, under considerable uncertainty. You can choose differently. My willingness to accept risk doesn't remove confidentiality obligations. I've described [my access choices](how-i-use-ai.md#choose-how-much-access-to-give-it) in more detail.

## “Is the setup worth it if it will soon be obsolete?”

I don't know how much of mine will last. My bet is that the help more than repays the investment in the short term. Working closely with these tools is also teaching me how they work and where they fail. I think that understanding will give me an edge with whatever comes next, even if some of today's setup becomes unnecessary.

The cost is real. Some recurring tasks took weeks to get working well. At first, explaining and correcting the job could take as long as doing it myself. I invested that time because I knew I'd need the help again.

Start with one real job you need to do. Build on it if it helps. Reproducing my whole setup would be a terrible first assignment. When an old instruction or process gets in the way, I ask AI to help work out whether to change it or get rid of it.

## “Will this help me focus, or give me more things to monitor?”

It cuts both ways. AI helps me get started. A revision can feel enormous, and opening the project to ask for a few concrete next steps is much easier than facing the whole thing at once. Even a bad first draft gives me something to react to. Arguing about what's wrong gets me moving.

Being able to advance several projects at once brings real productivity too. I can get work underway that I would otherwise keep putting off. Handing over some preparation also helps me put the task down for a while. But getting five projects moving is a very effective way to avoid spending two uninterrupted hours writing.

AI can keep me with that writing by quickly finding information I need or helping me work through an idea. It can also tempt me toward the easier progress available somewhere else. Getting help still leaves me to decide which project deserves my afternoon.

??? question "Where can I read about the bigger questions?"

    This page leaves larger questions about jobs, inequality, the environment and human learning mostly aside. These are useful places to read further. They offer different kinds of evidence and arguments, not a consensus.

    - **Jobs and inequality:** [David Autor's interview](https://issues.org/david-autor-economist-ai-future-work/) (January 2026) explains the opportunities and risks for workers. Compare [Yale's labor-market tracker](https://budgetlab.yale.edu/research/tracking-impact-ai-labor-market) (October 2026) with [Stanford's early employment indicators](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2026/10/ai-labor-market-economic-trends-data) (October 2026). They examine different data and groups, and the patterns do not settle what AI caused.
    - **Energy and environment:** [Hannah Ritchie at Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org/how-much-energy-do-data-centers-and-artificial-intelligence-use) (July 2026) separates AI from other data-center demand and puts individual queries in context. Long agent runs use more than a simple chat question, and global averages can hide local strain.
    - **Learning:** These explainers describe [a study of AI use in math practice](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education/) and [teacher-guided tutoring in Nigeria](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/education/From-chalkboards-to-chatbots-Transforming-learning-in-Nigeria). The settings differ. Neither establishes that AI always helps or harms learning.
    - **Power and governance:** [Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor's AI as Normal Technology](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/ai-as-normal-technology) (April 2025) emphasizes institutions and how technology gets adopted. It is a deliberately skeptical perspective on rapid-takeoff predictions.
